Motion FX Lab

Describe a video to an AI coding assistant, or write it yourself: it becomes a web page, and this repo renders the page to MP4, frame by frame. Nothing in the picture or the sound is AI-generated, so every frame is exact, editable like code and the same on every render. Every tile below is drawn live in your browser by the same code. Each card says what it does, how it is drawn and what it needs; filter them by kind or by the way they are drawn. A card marked “Seen in” is an effect we spotted in someone else's video and rebuilt from scratch. The sample text comes from our own videos for an Instagram DM assistant; swap in your own words. The original video pipeline uses 16 stacks; Flubber, PixiJS, Rapier and WebGPU/TSL extend the film-effect library.

Characters

Characters drawn in code

Four ways to draw a character in code: one agent character dithered to three inks, toon-shaded with an outline and drawn as flat SVG with a rig, plus a halftoned figure built from spheres and cylinders. No AI-generated art and no extra dependency; every pose, blink and hop is a function of t, so it renders frame-exact.

Dithered pixels, and a character that works a whole video

Seen in a TixFox promo by Berlin (@ox8erlin on Threads) and rebuilt on our own character: a two-pass render in flat colours and light, turned into three-colour pixels by a 4×4 ordered dither.

Toon, flat and halftone

What code does well

  • A clean app-mascot or vinyl-toy look.
  • Expressions, waves and hops driven by t; a new outfit or expression is a few lines.
  • One model in several styles: the toon and the dithered character are both drawn from agentModel().

Beyond code, still no AI art

  • An illustrator's layered SVG can be rigged part by part, the way the flat character is.
  • A 3D artist's Blender model, exported as GLB, loads with GLTFLoader, which ships with three.js r128.
  • Out of reach for code alone: an illustrator's line and personality, subtle expressions, real hair and cloth.

Rendering

From HTML to MP4, measured

Measured on a 56-second, 1,693-frame promo of ours with heavy 3D (the promo itself is not in this repo). Timed from launching the browsers to a finished lossless master. Our first pipeline, default headless Chromium with one browser and page.screenshot, took 399.6 s. The recorder now renders the whole video twice and compares every frame, in 52.9 s: 7.5× faster. Before that, with only an 8-frame spot check, it took 26.4 s. Encoding both deliverables with every check takes another 33.6 s. All measured on 2026-09-28 on an Apple M4 laptop.

Past 4 browsers there is no gain: the bottleneck moves to the single ffmpeg process that decodes the PNGs and writes the master.

What the checks caught on their first day: 4 runs stopped, no broken video shipped

  • A writer-loop bug: too few frames, stop.
  • Re-rendered frames differed: GPU edge noise on rotated text. The rule moved from byte-identical to a pixel tolerance; a wrong frame differs by 100,000+ pixels and still stops the run. Later, a spot check of 8 frames missed a browser that drew a label wrongly, so every frame is now rendered twice. The label had kept a stale size after a scale animation; engine.js now prevents that.
  • A decoder deadlock: the frame comparison, the frame counts and every delivery step now have a time limit, so a hang there fails loudly.
  • A bad argument: refused before starting.

Left out, and why

  • HEVC: the high-quality file has no size pressure and H.264 is enough; the web preview must play in every browser.
  • The GPU speed-up is verified on macOS only; without a Metal GPU, render with --cpu.
Not added

Stacks we looked at and left out

Later

  • three.js r128 → r186: the original effects keep r128; the three new film modules pin r180 without migrating existing shaders.
  • GSAP: not needed for these studies: Flubber handles the new concave outer-contour morph; the original polar morph remains available.
  • HyperFrames: the same approach as ours, but at v0.8 with its frame API marked experimental.

No

  • Remotion: videos are written as React components, and companies of more than three people need a paid licence.
  • lygia: commercial use needs a Patron licence.
  • Generative AI: no AI actors, footage, depth maps or music. Characters are drawn in code or by an illustrator, and music is synthesised in code.
